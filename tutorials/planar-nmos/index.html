Tutorials / Planar NMOS bench

A planar NMOS, from gate stack to delay, in the browser

This bench turns the layout and process numbers of a single planar NMOS (gate stack, gate length, doping, contact plugs) into resistance, capacitance, drive current, delay, leakage and breakdown. Everything runs as plain JavaScript in your browser; no server, no install.

Open the simulator → Run the verification tests
What it is and isn't. This is a teaching and trend-exploration tool built on textbook physics and example parameter values. It is not calibrated to any real process, and absolute numbers should not be used for design sign-off. The point is to see which knob moves which output, and why.

1. What it computes

The model is a compact model: closed-form equations, the same kind of thing circuit simulators use, rather than a field solver like TCAD. That makes one evaluation take well under a millisecond, which is what lets you drag a slider and see every output update, or let an optimizer try thousands of designs.

InputsOutputs

The default device is a 90 nm-class logic transistor (2 nm SiO2, VDD = 1.2 V). A switch at the top of the simulator changes to a 30 V-class high-voltage device (section 10).

2. Quick start

  1. Open the simulator. Inputs are on the left, results on the right, and a to-scale cross-section shows the device you are describing.
  2. Drag Gate length Lg down from 90 nm. Watch Vth fall and Ioff rise (short-channel effects) while CV/I improves.
  3. Raise Gate height. Cgg grows through the sidewall fringe, so CV/I gets slower, while Rg falls, so the gate RC improves. Two effects, opposite directions.
  4. In Parameter sweep, pick any input and any output to see the full curve, with a dashed line at the current value.
  5. In Design optimizer, switch on a few knobs, pick two goals (say Ioff and CV/I), add limits, and run. Click a point on the Pareto front to inspect it, apply it, or re-check it with the 2D solvers.
The delay outputs are in seconds, so a larger number is a slower device.

3. Gate stack and threshold voltage

A multi-layer gate dielectric is reduced to an equivalent SiO2 thickness, and the inversion capacitance adds the depth of the inversion charge and, for a poly gate, the depleted layer of the gate itself:

EOT = tIL·3.9/kIL + tHK·3.9/kHK    Tinv = EOT + 0.4 nm + tpoly-dep·3.9/11.7

The long-channel threshold is the textbook expression, with the body bias widening the depletion region:

Vth = VFB + 2φF + √(2qεSiNA(2φF + VSB)) / Cox,   VFB = φms − qQf/Cox

This is checked against an exact 1D solution of Poisson's equation (the integrated surface charge Qs(ψs)), and agrees to better than 0.01 mV, with and without body bias.

4. Short-channel effects

When the channel gets short, the source and drain take over part of the depletion charge under the gate, so Vth falls (roll-off), and the drain voltage lowers the source barrier (DIBL). The model uses the BSIM3 form:

ΔVth = −DVT0·θ(L)·(Vbi − 2φF) − ETA0·θDIBL(L)·VDS,   θ(L) = e−D·L/2λ + 2e−D·L/λ

with a characteristic length λ that grows with oxide thickness, depletion depth and the extension junction depth. The five constants were fitted to a 2D nonlinear Poisson solver written for this project, on a gate-length sweep, then checked on five held-out devices with different doping, oxide, junction depth and overlap. An earlier textbook version (fixed coefficients) under-predicted roll-off by 4–5× against the same 2D solution, which is why the fit was needed.

The subthreshold swing uses the Taur–Ning short-channel factor, so it also degrades at short L. Interface traps add qDit to the depletion capacitance in the swing.

5. Drain current

One smooth expression covers subthreshold to strong inversion (BSIM's effective gate overdrive Vgsteff), with a universal mobility curve and velocity saturation:

I = (W/L)·µeff·Cinv·Vgsteff·[1 − m·Vdseff/2(Vgsteff+2vt)]·Vdseff / (1 + Vdseff/EsatL)

Above saturation, a velocity-saturated region of length ΔL forms at the drain (channel-length modulation), and the current is multiplied by (1 + ΔL/L). Series resistance is applied self-consistently: the terminal current is the one for which the intrinsic transistor, seeing VGS − IRS and VDS − 2IRS, carries exactly that current.

Try the CLM strength input (0 to 2). At 1, the default device's Ion is about 27% higher than with CLM off, and CV/I is correspondingly faster. That coefficient is the textbook value and has not been fitted (section 13), so the slider lets you see how much the result depends on it.

6. Resistance and capacitance

Series resistance (per side)

  • Front end: where the channel meets the extension, current crowds through the overlap. The gate-induced accumulation layer and the extension form two coupled resistive sheets (a two-layer transmission line), in parallel with the spreading resistance at the gate edge. The coupling coefficient was calibrated against a 2D resistor solver over 27 combinations.
  • Extension and deep S/D sheets along the path to the plug, contact resistance from thermionic-field emission (ρc = ρc0·exp(φB/E0)), and the plug body.

A nice consequence: RS first falls as the overlap grows (less spreading), then rises again (the overlap itself has resistance). The 2D solver shows the same minimum.

Capacitance

  • Channel and overlap from Cinv and Cox.
  • Outer fringe from the gate sidewall through spacer then ILD (quarter-circle field lines), cut off by a grounded contact plug only over the width the plugs actually cover.
  • Plug-to-gate plate plus edge fringes, with the spacer and ILD in series.
  • Junctions as one-sided abrupt junctions, averaged over the 0 → VDD drain swing.

Fringe and plug–gate capacitance agree with a 2D Laplace solver within about 16% over eleven geometries.

Ieff is the average of the currents at (VDD, VDD/2) and (VDD/2, VDD), a better proxy for switching than Ion alone.

8. Leakage

  • Subthreshold from the same current equation at VGS = 0.
  • Gate direct tunnelling: Schuegraf–Hu for the SiO2 layer with a WKB factor for a high-k layer. 1.5 nm SiO2 at 1 V gives about 3.5 A/cm², in the range of published measurements, and the current drops about one decade per 0.2 nm.
  • Junction band-to-band tunnelling and GIDL at the drain edge (order-of-magnitude).
  • SRH generation in the drain depletion volume, q·ni·Wdep·A/(2τg), which dominates at high temperature and high voltage.

The simulator shows which component dominates. For the default device it is subthreshold; below about 1.4 nm oxide, gate tunnelling takes over; above about 6×1018 cm−3 channel doping, junction tunnelling does.

9. Breakdown and hot carriers

Breakdown is computed for each mechanism separately, and the lowest one sets BVdss (gate, source and body grounded). The simulator draws all of them as bars, so you see not only the number but what limits it.

  • Junction avalanche: the ionisation integral ∫α dx = 1 with Van Overstraeten–de Man coefficients, over the field of a cylindrical junction edge (radius = junction depth). Within 1% of a full 1D Poisson field profile with the same coefficients.
  • Gated-diode avalanche: the same integral with the gate-induced vertical field added at the drain edge.
  • Zener and GIDL: the tunnelling current reaches a criterion current (1 µA/µm by default; it is an input, since every company uses its own spec).
  • Punch-through: the VGS = 0 channel current reaches the criterion. Within about 10% of the 2D Poisson solver for short channels; one longer, more heavily doped case is 49% high and is among the known failures (section 12).
  • Hot carriers and snapback: substrate current from Hu's model; snapback when Isub·Rsub forward-biases the source junction by 0.7 V.

Against a 2D Poisson solution with ionisation integrals taken along field lines, the compact avalanche voltage agrees within about 20% on the devices tested.

10. The 30 V-class HV device

High-voltage transistors (for example the ones that pass program and erase voltages in non-volatile memory) look different: a thick oxide (here 65 nm), a long channel, and a drain built to spread the field.

  • Three-layer drain: a shallow, lightly doped drift (LDD) from under the gate; an n+ placed a drift length away from the gate edge; and a deep, lightly doped layer (DDD) wrapping the n+ and reaching toward the gate.
  • Drift resistance: parallel sheets, thinned by the drift–body depletion at the local bias (a JFET effect), with velocity saturation capped at the Kirk current q·N·vsat·t, plus space-charge-limited conduction beyond it. The low-current value agrees with a 2D resistor within 4% over twelve structures.
  • Breakdown at three places: the gate edge and drift surface (a quasi-2D surface Poisson model including the gate field and the depletion from the body below, the RESURF effect), the LDD corner, and the bottom corner of the n+/DDD. Against 103 2D Poisson + ionisation runs, the error is 7–17% rms depending on the data set.
  • Pass voltage: with the gate at VDD, the output can only rise until VG − Vout = Vth(VSB + Vout); the body effect is what makes this matter.
  • Field transistor: poly running over the isolation oxide can invert the surface between neighbouring n+ regions; its threshold is shown and flagged if close to VDD.
  • Ron·A (on-resistance × footprint), the standard figure of merit traded against breakdown voltage.

The example HV device gives BVdss ≈ 37 V (limited at the n+/DDD corner; a live 2D run gives 39 V), Vth ≈ 0.92 V and Ron·A ≈ 149 mΩ·mm². The values are illustrative, not from any real process.

11. Design optimizer

The optimizer is NSGA-II, a genetic algorithm that keeps a population of designs and evolves it toward the Pareto front: the set of designs where you cannot improve one goal without making the other worse.

  • Toggle which inputs it may change; everything else stays at your current values.
  • Pick one or two goals and any number of limits (e.g. Vth ≥ 0.25 V, oxide field ≤ 6 MV/cm, BV ≥ 1.2·VDD).
  • Optionally require robustness to gate-length variation: each candidate is judged at its worst ±ΔL corner.
  • Limits that none of the selected knobs can move are detected before the run and reported, instead of silently blocking it.

Optimizers are good at finding where a model is weakest, so a chosen design can be re-checked against the 2D solvers with one click, and it is flagged if it sits outside the range where the fitted parts of the model were checked.

12. How it was checked

The guiding rule: compare against something that does not reuse the model's own equations, and write down the expected result and tolerance before running. Several independent reference solvers were written for this:

ReferenceUsed for
Exact 1D MOS charge (integrated Poisson)Threshold, body effect, inversion charge
1D nonlinear Poisson for a pn junctionJunction capacitance, avalanche field profile
2D Laplace (finite volume)Gate fringe and plug-to-gate capacitance
2D resistorOverlap/spreading resistance, HV drift resistance
2D nonlinear Poisson (LV and HV cross-sections)Short-channel effects, punch-through, avalanche along field lines

On top of that, a trend matrix: for every input, the expected direction of every output (up, down, or no change) was written from physics before running the model, then checked over a sweep. It has 56 rows and 386 graded cells. Writing the expectations first is what caught most of the real errors, such as a missing short-channel term in the swing, or a capacitance that rose when a grounded plug moved away from the gate.

The full suite has 302 tests: 293 pass, 5 report a number without a pass/fail criterion, and 4 fail. The failures are known model limitations, kept visible rather than tuned away. You can run the whole suite yourself on the test page (the quick run takes about a minute).

13. Known limitations

  • Inversion charge is over-estimated by 25–45% in strong inversion compared with the exact 1D solution, because threshold is defined at ψs = 2φF. Ion is likely high by a similar amount.
  • The CLM coefficient is not fitted. Poisson's equation fixes charge and potential, not how carriers move, so it cannot calibrate saturation current or output conductance; that would need a drift-diffusion solver or measured I–V curves.
  • No calibration to a real process. Mobility, strain, halo profiles and quantum effects are textbook or absent. The fitted constants are fitted to this project's own 2D solvers, which use rectangular junctions and uniform doping.
  • HV device: no short-channel current model (punch-through is reported as a conservative 1D bound instead), and no impact ionisation in the drift region, so on-state safe operating area is not covered.
  • Heavily doped (1020) silicon resistivity is 22% low against Masetti's data, and the avalanche coefficients give a breakdown 36% above Sze's empirical fit at 1017 cm−3.

Treat outputs as trend and sensitivity estimates. If you have measured or TCAD I–V curves, they are the right thing to calibrate against.
